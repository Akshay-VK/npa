# Reactive potentials and long-timescale methods

A potential that is accurate on equilibrium structures is not a potential that
can model a reaction. This document covers the two things that stand between
the MLIP state of the art and a working reaction surrogate: **data that
contains bond breaking**, and **methods that can reach the timescales on which
reactions happen**. It is the closest prior art to what this project is trying
to build.

**Bottom line**

- Transition1x is the canonical reactive dataset: **9.6 million DFT
  calculations on 10,000 reactions at ωB97x/6-31G(d)**, generated by nudged
  elastic band, covering H/C/N/O with up to seven heavy atoms.
- It is made of *path optimisations*, not dynamics. That distinction is the
  single most important caveat in this document.
- Training on equilibrium data does not teach barrier physics; Transition1x's
  authors demonstrate this directly.
- Long-timescale methods (metadynamics, umbrella sampling, replica exchange)
  are how the field reaches millisecond events with a femtosecond potential.
  They are complementary to a fast surrogate, not replaced by it.
- A model can have excellent force accuracy and still get rates wrong by orders
  of magnitude. Kinetic accuracy is a separate, harder target.

## Transition1x

Schreiner, Bhowmik, Vegge, Busk and Winther, *Transition1x — a dataset for
building generalizable reactive machine learning potentials*, Scientific Data 9,
2022, [arXiv:2207.12858](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12858).

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Configurations | 9.6 million |
| Reactions | ~10,000 |
| Level of theory | ωB97x / 6-31G(d) |
| Elements | H, C, N, O |
| Size | up to 7 heavy atoms, up to 6 bond changes |
| Generation | Nudged elastic band, saving intermediate calculations |
| Content | Reactant, transition state, product, and the path between |

The construction matters. For each reaction, the authors ran NEB — an
optimisation that finds a minimum-energy path between two known endpoints — and
saved every intermediate image. The dataset is therefore a dense sampling of
the *reaction path*, including the saddle region, which is exactly what
equilibrium MD datasets lack.

The authors then trained equivariant graph message-passing networks on it and
cross-validated on ANI-1x and QM9. Their conclusion is blunt: "ML models cannot
learn features in transition-state regions solely by training on hitherto
popular benchmark datasets."

That is the empirical foundation for this entire project. If equilibrium data
were sufficient, there would be no problem to solve.

### The caveat that changes everything

NEB images are **optimised geometries**, not trajectories. They are the result
of a constrained minimisation at zero temperature, with the path discretised
into a fixed number of images.

Consequences for anyone training a dynamics model:

1. **No velocities.** You cannot learn an integrator from static images.
2. **No time coordinate.** The path parameter is arc length, not femtoseconds.
3. **No thermal fluctuations.** Real reactions at 500 K explore a distribution
   around the path; NEB gives one representative.
4. **Sampling bias.** The images cluster near the path, which is a
   measure-zero set of configuration space.
5. **Level of theory.** ωB97x/6-31G(d) is a modest hybrid with a small basis.
   Barrier heights from it carry real uncertainty.

*(inference)* Point 3 is the most under-appreciated. A model trained on NEB
images learns the *minimum-energy path*, which is the most probable path but not
the only one. Rates depend on the full transition-state ensemble, including
entropic contributions, and a path-trained model has never seen that ensemble.

> **In plain words.** The dataset shows the easiest route over the mountain,
> surveyed at absolute zero, with no weather. Real reactions happen in a storm,
> and they sometimes cross somewhere else. That is still enormously better than
> having no map at all.

## What a reactive potential actually needs

Four requirements, in order of how often they are violated:

**1. Coverage of the saddle region.** Achieved by path data (Transition1x) or
by enhanced sampling.

**2. Correct dissociation limits.** As bonds stretch to infinity, the energy
must approach the sum of fragment energies. Most local models fail this because
they never see separated fragments.

**3. Correct spin and charge states.** Bond breaking often changes the
electronic state. A model with a single implicit state cannot represent a
singlet–triplet crossing.

**4. Smoothness.** Forces must be continuous, or the integrator will explode.
Neural networks with ReLU activations are only piecewise smooth; this is a
real engineering constraint.

Requirement 2 is why "reactive" potentials often add explicit long-range and
fragment-aware terms rather than relying on a bigger network.

> **In plain words.** A reaction model has to know what happens when a bond
> stretches all the way to nothing, has to know which electron state it is in,
> and has to be smooth enough that the simulation does not explode. Those are
> three separate engineering problems.

## The training-signal problem

There are four qualitatively different ways to supervise a reaction model, and
they produce different models:

| Signal | What it teaches | Data source | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Energies at configurations** | The PES | DFT single points | Learns nothing about dynamics |
| **Forces at configurations** | The PES gradient | DFT forces | Same; forces are still static |
| **Path / barrier heights** | Where the saddle is | NEB, IRC | Path-biased; no off-path behaviour |
| **Trajectories** | The actual evolution | AIMD, MLIP-MD | Expensive; rare events scarce |

The source note's instinct — "train on time series of chemical reactions" — is
the fourth row, which is the most expensive and the least available. The prior
art mostly uses rows 1–3, and gets away with it because a potential is a
*function of configuration*, not a function of time. If you learn the function
well enough, trajectories follow.

This is the deepest strategic question in the project: **is a trajectory loss
necessary, or is a good PES sufficient?** The entire MLIP field bets on the
latter. NPA's direct-update variant bets on the former. The energy-based hybrid
can have both.

> **In plain words.** You can learn chemistry by looking at still photographs
> of molecules (energies and forces), or by watching films (trajectories).
> Photographs are cheap and plentiful; films are expensive and rare. The bet
> everyone else makes is that enough photographs let you predict the film.

## Long-timescale methods

Even a perfect potential cannot simulate a millisecond event at 1 fs steps —
that is $10^{12}$ steps. The field's answer is to change the sampling, not the
potential.

| Method | Idea | Cost | Gives rates? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metadynamics** | Add history-dependent bias along collective variables to escape basins | Moderate | Yes, with reweighting |
| **Umbrella sampling** | Biased windows along a coordinate, then unbiased | Moderate | Yes, free-energy profile |
| **Replica exchange** | Simulate many temperatures, swap configurations | High | Yes, thermodynamics |
| **Transition path sampling** | Sample reactive paths without a bias | High | Yes, mechanism |
| **Hyperdynamics** | Add a bias that accelerates escapes without changing the mechanism | Low | Yes, with care |
| **Temperature acceleration** | Run hot, extrapolate | Low | Approximate |
| **Machine-learned CVs** | Learn the slow coordinate | Moderate | Yes |

The important point for this project: **a fast surrogate and an enhanced
sampling method are multiplicative, not competing.** If your potential is 1000×
faster, you can afford 1000× more sampling, which is exactly what rare-event
problems need. That is the strongest practical argument for the whole
enterprise, and it does not depend on the NPA architecture at all.

*(inference)* It also means the project's evaluation should include a
sampling-based experiment, not just single-point accuracy: run metadynamics on
a small reaction with the learned potential and with DFT, and compare the free
energy profile. That is a much more convincing result than a force MAE.

> **In plain words.** You cannot watch a millisecond reaction one femtosecond at
> a time. So you cheat: heat it up, push it along, run many copies. A faster
> model lets you cheat harder. That is the real payoff.

## Why low force error does not imply correct rates

This deserves its own subsection because it is the most common error in the
literature.

Rates depend on the barrier through the Eyring equation, roughly
$k \propto \exp(-\Delta E^\ddagger / k_B T)$. At 500 K, $k_B T \approx 1$ kcal/mol,
so:

| Barrier error | Rate error factor |
|---|---|
| 0.1 kcal/mol | ~1.1× |
| 0.5 kcal/mol | ~1.6× |
| 1.0 kcal/mol | ~2.7× |
| 2.0 kcal/mol | ~7.4× |
| 5.0 kcal/mol | ~150× |

A model with a force MAE of 20 meV/Å — which sounds excellent — can easily have
a 2 kcal/mol barrier error, and therefore be wrong about the rate by an order of
magnitude. Force accuracy and barrier accuracy are correlated but not
equivalent: the barrier is a *difference* between two special points, and
errors at those points need not cancel.

This is the core argument for evaluating on barriers and rates, not just on
forces. See [metrics](../05-evaluation/02-metrics-and-statistics.md).

> **In plain words.** A tiny error in the height of the hill becomes a huge
> error in how often molecules cross it, because the crossing rate depends
> exponentially on the height. Being 10% wrong about forces can make you 10×
> wrong about speed.

## What exists, and what does not

**Exists and is usable now:**

- Transition1x for path data (H/C/N/O, small molecules).
- OMol25 for broad pretraining, including reactive structures.
- Universal potentials (UMA, eSEN) as baselines and teachers.
- Standard enhanced-sampling tools (PLUMED, ASE, OpenMM) that accept any
  energy/force function.

**Does not exist, and is the project's opportunity:**

- A standard benchmark for **reactive dynamics** at scale. There is no
  "ImageNet of reaction trajectories".
- A public dataset of **time-resolved reactive trajectories** with forces, at
  a quality comparable to Transition1x.
- A accepted metric for **kinetic accuracy** of a learned potential.
- A model that combines **persistent state** with a conservative energy.

*(inference)* Building the benchmark may be a more valuable contribution than
building the model. It is also a defensible first paper: unglamorous,
reproducible, and immediately useful to everyone else in the field.

> **In plain words.** There is no standard exam for "can your model predict how
> fast a reaction goes". Making that exam might be more useful than being the
> first to pass it.

## Implications for the project

1. **Use Transition1x as the first real dataset**, but understand it is path
   data. Do not claim trajectory accuracy from it.
2. **Evaluate on barriers before trajectories.** It is cheaper and more
   discriminating.
3. **Include an enhanced-sampling experiment** once a potential works. It is
   the clearest demonstration of value.
4. **Do not build a reactive dataset from scratch** unless the project
   explicitly chooses the benchmark contribution. It is a multi-month effort.
5. **Check the level of theory.** ωB97x/6-31G(d) is not a gold standard; a
   model that matches it is matching a modest method.

> **In plain words.** Start with the dataset that exists, judge yourself on the
> hill heights first, and only then run the expensive sampling experiment. Do
> not spend six months making a new dataset before you know the model works.

## Open questions

- Is there a public reactive *trajectory* dataset at Transition1x scale that
  this search missed? If so, it changes the data plan entirely.
- How much does the 6-31G(d) basis inflate Transition1x barrier errors relative
  to a def2-TZVP reference?
- Can a model trained on NEB paths reproduce an AIMD free-energy profile, or
  does the path bias show up quantitatively?

## What would change this document

- Release of a large reactive-trajectory dataset, which would make the
  trajectory-supervision question testable at scale.
- A published demonstration that path-trained potentials get rates right,
  which would strengthen the "PES is sufficient" bet.
- Evidence that barrier errors and force errors are strongly correlated after
  all, which would simplify evaluation considerably.
